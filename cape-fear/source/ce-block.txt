  <summary><div class="callhead"><span class="line">Line 1</span><h2>What does CAPE measure?</h2><span class="stage">The ruler</span><span class="ans"><span class="ansv">Price ÷ ten years of profits</span><span class="chev" aria-hidden="true"></span></span></div></summary>
  <div class="reply">
    <div class="answer">
      <span class="say">The number you require is</span>
      <div class="big g" style="font-size:clamp(34px,5vw,56px);line-height:1.05">Price over ten years of profits</div>
      <p>Take the S&amp;P 500, adjusted for inflation. Divide it by the average of the last ten years of company earnings, also adjusted for inflation. The result says how many years of average profit the market is paying for. Averaging over ten years smooths out booms and recessions, so one unusually good or bad year cannot swing it.</p>
      <p>John Campbell and Robert Shiller set it out in 1988. Shiller still publishes the series every month, back to 1881.</p>
    </div>
    <div class="fig">
      <h3>The sum, October 2026</h3>

<!-- 4 THE CASE AGAINST -->
<details class="call" id="ce-against">
  <summary><div class="callhead"><span class="line">Line 4</span><h2>Why might the ruler mislead?</h2><span class="stage">The case against</span><span class="ans"><span class="ansv">Partly, not wholly</span><span class="chev" aria-hidden="true"></span></span></div></summary>
  <div class="reply one">
    <p class="both">Serious people argue that today's CAPE <em>overstates</em> how expensive the market is. Serious people argue it <em>still</em> carries information. Both cases are stated here by the people who make them.</p>
    <div class="two">
      <div>
        <span class="who">The case that the ruler reads high · critics</span>
        <h3>The "normal" level has moved up</h3>
        <ul>
          <li><strong>Accounting changed.</strong> Rules adopted in the early 2000s make companies write down the value of assets they still hold, but not write it up. That pushes reported earnings down in bad years, which depresses the ten-year average and lifts CAPE (Jeremy Siegel, Financial Analysts Journal, 2016).</li>
          <li><strong>Less is paid out.</strong> Companies now return more cash through buybacks and less through dividends. Earnings per share then grow faster, and a faster-growing stream can justify a higher multiple.</li>
          <li><strong>The average lags.</strong> When earnings have grown fast, a ten-year average sits well below today's profits, so CAPE reads higher than a one-year ratio would.</li>
          <li><strong>The market has changed shape.</strong> Today's index is weighted towards software and platform companies with higher profit margins than the railways and manufacturers of a century ago.</li>
        </ul>
      </div>
      <div>
        <span class="who">The case that it still carries information · defenders</span>
        <h3>Adjust for all that, and it still reads high</h3>
        <ul>
          <li><strong>The adjustments narrow the gap, not close it.</strong> Clifford Asness and colleagues at AQR tested the main objections and found CAPE still high against its own history after allowing for them ("An Old Friend: The Stock Market's Shiller P/E", 2012).</li>
          <li><strong>The low-rate excuse is weaker now.</strong> High multiples were often defended by very low bond yields. The 10-year Treasury par yield was 4.91% on 2 Nov 2026, against under 1% in 2020 (Treasury).</li>
          <li><strong>The pattern held through the changes.</strong> Line 5 shows the step-down in later returns across more than a century that included several changes in accounting and payout habits.</li>
        </ul>
      </div>
    </div>
